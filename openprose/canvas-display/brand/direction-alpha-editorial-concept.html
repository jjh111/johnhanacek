A field note from the operations floor

On the quiet arrival of Monday reports.

For three years we ran a meeting whose only function was to confirm that the dashboards were fine. Then we declared the outcome in a file, and the meeting got fifteen minutes shorter. This is what changed.

Sarah K. Director of Operations · Ridgeline Properties (40 buildings, NJ & PA) Filed Monday, May 4 · 09:14:42 ET
— the meeting nobody could justify but nobody could cancel ↓

Once, the week began with a scramble. Someone — usually an analyst named Jamie who had been the youngest hire on the team three years running — opened seven dashboards on a Sunday night, pulled the numbers, wrote a digest, and dropped it in the 09:00 standup on Monday morning. The rotation was a line in the budget. The standup was a line in the calendar. Nobody was quite sure what either accomplished beyond confirming that the dashboards were fine1.

I am not the first person to make this observation, and I will not be the last. There is a particular flavor of meeting that exists only because nobody on the calendar invite remembers who scheduled it2. The Monday brief, in our case, was that meeting. We knew we wanted the digest. We were not sure we wanted the standup that wrapped around the digest like a cardboard sleeve around a coffee cup.

Then, in the third week of last March, we declared the outcome in a file. The file was fifteen lines long. It said: every Monday at 09:00 ET, deliver a one-page brief to the operations Slack channel; cite all sources; flag any building running more than 12% over its annual energy budget. We checked it into the repo, the way we check anything else into the repo. The file ran on cadence. The brief arrived. The dashboards stayed fine.

The first thing we noticed was what we stopped doing. The standing meetings where we'd review the dashboard to confirm the dashboard was fine.
↑ Jamie says this is true; he didn't have to come in early on Sundays anymore

The standup happens anyway, because it is a Monday morning and people want to see each other's faces, but it is fifteen minutes shorter and nobody opens a dashboard during it. The numbers are already in the document. We argue about the numbers, which is a better use of the meeting than confirming them3.

I want to be careful about a claim that sounds bigger than it is. We did not eliminate Jamie's job. We freed him from the part of his job that nobody was paying him to do well — the rote pulling-of-numbers. He spends Mondays in the field now, in buildings, looking at boilers. The boilers are not in any dashboard yet. They will be eventually. The dashboards we currently have are the dashboards we currently know how to ask for. There is more, always.

* * *

What changed in our particular operation was not the technology. The technology was always there — APIs, schedulers, summarization models, the whole boilerplate of a modern back office. What changed was the willingness to write down what we wanted in a single small file and trust the runtime to keep its end of the contract every Monday at 09:00 ET, forever, until we change the file.

The contract is the part most people miss when they describe this kind of work. It is not the schedule, and it is not the prompt. It is the line that says: if the digest does not arrive, declare a failure, page the on-call, and refund the line item. That line, more than any other line in the file, is what made me trust the system to run on a Monday I was not paying attention to.

I should not have to pay attention every Monday. That is the point.

By the eighth week, the brief had become canonical — the document Jamie used to write was the document the runtime now wrote, and the editorial corrections we made on the runtime's drafts started to make their way back into the file. The file got smarter on a weekly cadence, the same way a junior writer does after eight weeks of edits. By v42, the file knew our voice.

We renamed Jamie's role last month. The job description used to be three paragraphs of dashboard-pulling and one paragraph of "other duties as assigned." It now reads, in its entirety: maintain the responsibilities the operations team has declared, and declare new ones as the team needs them. It is a one-line job description for a one-line file. We pay him more.

Notes

  1. The dashboards were fine, in fact, every single Monday morning of the rotation. A version of this same essay could be titled How to know your dashboards are fine without holding a meeting about it. (See also Tufte, Beautiful Evidence, on the dashboard as “a graphic that organizes a large collection of numbers and makes comparisons between different parts of the data and tells a story.” The Monday standup was none of these things.)
  2. Jenny Odell calls these meetings “the calendar's vestigial organs” in Saving Time. I would call them something less polite, but I am writing this on company letterhead.
  3. This is the part of the change I did not predict and would not trade away. A meeting whose only function is to confirm the numbers is not a meeting; it is a status check that for some reason eats coffee. A meeting that argues about the numbers is the actual work of running a portfolio.
