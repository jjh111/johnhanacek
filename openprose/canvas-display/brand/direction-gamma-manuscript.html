I · Palette

Cocoa and cream, with a single thread of gold.

Two materials and one ornament. Cocoa #1e160c is the dramatic ground — the masthead, the spec plate, the dark variation. Cream #f3ead8 is the manuscript leaf, the surface every word of prose is set on. Between them runs gold #c89b3c — the only accent — in italic, in section markers, in hairline rules, in the drop-cap that opens every section. No editorial red. No highlighter. The page is dignified by what it leaves out.

II · Type Scale

Cormorant for the prose. DM Sans for the chrome.

Cormorant Garamond carries every running paragraph, every heading, every long sentence. Its display weights cut a wider stroke than Garamond proper, and its italic carries the gold accent — the page's only chromatic mark. DM Sans, all caps and small, handles the chrome at a whisper: kickers, button faces, badge labels. JetBrains Mono handles code and the spec plate. Italic is always gold. The drop-cap is always gold. Nothing else is.

V · The Drop Cap

Italic is the writer. The drop-cap is the manuscript's invitation to begin.

Every section opens with a five-line gold drop-cap on the first paragraph. It is the manuscript's only persistent ornament — the ceremony of starting again. Italic gold is the author's emphasis inside a paragraph, used sparingly. Together they are the entire emphasis system: the drop-cap announces the beginning, the italic marks the turning sentence. There is nothing else.

The drop · cap
Every Monday at 09:00 ET, the digest arrives — verified, signed, archived. The standup happens anyway, but it is fifteen minutes shorter.
The italic · pivot
Billed by delivery. If the outcome doesn't ship, no line. The italic is not for emphasis on a phrase; it is for the word the sentence turns on.
The two together
The humanist computer is the insight that these two dispositions — craft and machinery — are not opposed. The drop opens; the italic turns.
Rule · the drop Use on the first paragraph of every prose section. Five lines tall, gold deep, no ornament. The first letter of the section is always a drop. There is no exception.
Rule · italic Use on the single word the sentence turns on — not for emphasis on a phrase, but for the pivot in the meaning. Always gold. Never two italics in one sentence.
Rule · layering Drop-cap and italic never share a paragraph — the drop already opens the paragraph; the italic would dilute. The drop's letter is never an italic letter.
Rule · the mark Never on the logotype. Never on a button. The drop-cap is a manuscript tool only — body copy, editorial first paragraphs, document preview body. Not chrome.
VI · Editorial Pairing

Set in type — the voice at length, beginning with a drop.

The manuscript voice is calm, specific, and ceremonial about its beginnings. It opens with a drop-cap, names things by their nouns, and reaches for italic only when italic carries the pivot. The whole thing reads as if a careful editor has set the page.

From the field notes

On the quiet arrival of Monday reports.

Once, the week began with a scramble. Someone opened seven dashboards, pulled the numbers, wrote the digest, and dropped it in the 09:00 standup. The rotation was a line in the budget. The standup was a line in the calendar. Nobody was quite sure what either accomplished beyond confirming that the dashboards were fine.

Then the outcome was declared in a file. Fifteen lines. Every Monday at 09:00 ET, the digest arrives — verified, signed, archived. The standup happens anyway, but it is fifteen minutes shorter. Nobody asks where the numbers came from. They are already in the document.

The first thing we noticed was what we stopped doing — the standing meetings where we'd review the dashboard to confirm the dashboard was fine.

The humanist computer is the insight that these two dispositions — craft and machinery — are not opposed. The machine handles the cadence. The prose handles the voice. The page reads the way a good analyst would have written it. The result is the point.

VIII · Voice & Tone

Three sentences, one register, three placements.

Three sanctioned voices the brand uses: the short declaration, the spec-sheet note, the editorial observation. Each sentence can live on its own without ornament — and each demonstrates one placement of italic gold.

Voice · short · rule
“Write the outcome. We run it.”

The tagline. Six words. Italic on the second sentence — the part that shifts the burden from the reader to the service. No drop here; the tagline is too short to earn one.

Voice · spec · pivot
“Billed by delivery. If the outcome doesn't ship, no line.”

Terms of art spoken plainly. Italic gold on the one word that defines the pricing model — the pivot of the entire promise. The italic is the only mark.

Voice · editorial · italic
“Once, the week began with a scramble. Then the outcome was declared in a file.”

The long form voice. Italic carries the pivot. In a longer setting this sentence would open with a drop on its first paragraph — here it sits as a pull-quote, so the drop is held back.
