Chapter I

The Burden of Attention

*

Once there was a company that grew faster than its people could follow. Every week brought new tasks that someone had to tend: monitor the sensors, compile the reports, watch the dashboards, alert the stakeholders. Each task was small. Together, they were a weight that pressed down on every hour of every day. The people were not lazy. They were simply finite.

They hired more people. They bought more tools. Each solution demanded its own management, its own learning curve, its own slice of the day. The tools multiplied. The burden did not shrink. It changed shape.

A cell divides. Each daughter cell divides again. This is the nature of operational work: it reproduces. Tools do not eliminate it. They give it new surfaces on which to grow. On the arithmetic of attention
Chapter II

Two Kinds of Delegation

*

There are two ways to delegate. The first is to stand beside someone and explain every step. Check their work. Correct their mistakes. This is how most agent tools work today: you prescribe the task, inspect the output, iterate, repeat. You remain the manager. Your attention stays consumed.

The second way is different. You describe what good looks like. You set the boundaries. Then you leave the room. This is how you work with a senior employee: someone who understands the why, not just the what. You do not check on them. They check in with you, only when they have something to report.

The Imperative Way

Tell it what to do. Check the output. Iterate. Repeat. You remain the manager. Your attention is the bottleneck.

The Declarative Way

Describe the outcome. Set the contract. Let it run. A brief arrives in Slack. You never think about it again.

Declarative approaches (SQL, Terraform, React) have consistently won in domains where what matters more than how. Agent orchestration is the next domain. On the history of abstraction
Chapter III

The Book of Responsibilities

*

And so they built a different kind of system. Not a tool. Not an assistant. A book of responsibilities: a living document where you write down what needs to happen, what good looks like, and what the guardrails are. The book keeps itself up.

No dashboard to check. No tool to learn. You write the responsibility in plain language. The system reads it, understands the contract, and begins. Results arrive on cadence, in Slack or email. Like a colleague who has been doing this work for years. Quietly. Reliably. Without being asked.

Chapter V

The Moral

*

Every fable ends with a lesson. This one is simple. The scarcest resource in any organization is not money, not time, not talent. It is attention. And attention, once fragmented, does not reassemble itself.

The tools we build should not demand more of it. They should return it. That is the promise of declarative work: describe the outcome, set the contract, and get your attention back.
