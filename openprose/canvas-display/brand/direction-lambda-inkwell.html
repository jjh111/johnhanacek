I · Palette

Ivory paper, sepia ink, and a thread of warm rust.

Three colors, one mark. The paper is ivory — warmer and lighter than parchment, the surface of a fresh page. The ink is sepia, never pure black: the brand is a hand-drawn line, and pure black would betray the gesture. Warm rust carries every italic accent and every active stroke. The single highlighter is a soft rust wash — the writer's pen, not the reader's. The mark itself is the only visual chrome.

II · Type Scale

Cormorant Garamond for the voice. Inter for labels.

Cormorant Garamond italic carries the prose and the display — the same hand-drawn feeling as the mark, set in type. Inter handles the micro-labels and UI primitives; it's the calm voice of the chrome. JetBrains Mono is reserved for the spec block and the run log. Italic rust is the writer's emphasis; the wash highlighter sits beneath the writer, never beneath the reader. One hand throughout.

V · The Highlighter

Italic is the writer. Highlighter is a full-block reader's pen.

One new rule for v2. Italic red is how the author places emphasis — authorial voice, thesis words, the place where the sentence turns. Yellow highlighter is how the reader marks what to act on. They sit on different layers of the same page and never fight for the same phrase.

Rule · yellow Use on 1–2 short phrases per paragraph. The phrases a reader in a hurry needs to see. Never on verbs, always on nouns + numbers.
Rule · hot Use sparingly on a single claim per page — the proof point, the promise, the pivot. Two hot marks on one page loses the urgency.
Rule · layering Italic red and highlighter never share a phrase. The writer and the reader each get their own words. Crossing them reads as shouting.
Rule · the mark Never on the logotype. Never on a button. The highlighter is a prose tool only — body copy, editorial H2/H3, document preview headers. Not chrome.
VI · The Voice in Long Form

Set in type — the voice at length, with a reader's pen.

The humanist-computer voice is calm, specific, and unafraid of a short sentence. It names things by their nouns. It reaches for italic only when italic is warranted. And it trusts the reader with a highlighter.

From the field notes

On the quiet arrival of Monday reports.

Once, the week began with a scramble. Someone opened seven dashboards, pulled the numbers, wrote the digest, and dropped it in the 09:00 standup. The rotation was a line in the budget. The standup was a line in the calendar. Nobody was quite sure what either accomplished beyond confirming that the dashboards were fine.

Then the outcome was declared in a file. Fifteen lines. Every Monday at 09:00 ET, the digest arrives — verified, signed, archived. The standup happens anyway, but it is fifteen minutes shorter. Nobody asks where the numbers came from. They are already in the document.

The first thing we noticed was what we stopped doing — the standing meetings where we'd review the dashboard to confirm the dashboard was fine.

The humanist computer is the insight that these two dispositions — craft and machinery — are not opposed. The machine handles the cadence. The prose handles the voice. The page reads the way a good analyst would have written it. The result is the point.

Maya Chen · Head of Operations · Series-C payments platform

VIII · Voice & Tone

Three sentences, one register, three marks.

Three sanctioned voices the brand uses: the short declaration, the spec-sheet note, the editorial observation. Each sentence can live on its own without ornament — and each demonstrates one placement of the highlighter.

Voice · short · yellow
“Write the outcome. We run it.”

The tagline. Six words. The highlighter lands on the second sentence — the part that shifts the burden from the reader to the service.

Voice · spec · hot
“Billed by delivery. If the outcome doesn't ship, no line.”

Terms of art spoken plainly. Hot mark on the one word that defines the pricing model — the claim a reader cannot skim past.

Voice · editorial · italic
“Once, the week began with a scramble. Then the outcome was declared in a file.”

The long form voice. Italic carries the pivot. No highlighter here — this sentence is still the author's, not yet the reader's.
