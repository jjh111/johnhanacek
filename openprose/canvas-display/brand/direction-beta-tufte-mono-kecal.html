I · Palette

Paper, ink, and a four-step grayscale.

No editorial red. No highlighter. Tufte trusts the page itself, and the page is off-white. Ink is true black. Between them sits a four-step grayscale — from secondary copy down to the faintest hairline rule — that does the entire job of hierarchy. The accent is invisibility: the page is loud only by the absence of color.

II · Type Scale

EB Garamond for the prose. Inter for the chrome. JetBrains for the numbers.

Three faces, three jobs, no overlap. EB Garamond carries every running paragraph, every heading, every long sentence. Inter, all caps and small, sets only the labels and the chrome — eyebrows, kickers, button faces. JetBrains Mono handles code, sidenote markers, and any place where a numeral matters more than the word next to it. Italic is the writer's only emphasis; the sidenote is the reader's.

III · The Sidenote

Italic is the writer. The sidenote is the reader's room to disagree.

Tufte's defining gesture: a numbered marginal note that earns its space because the reader can choose to read it or not. Italic is how the author places emphasis — authorial voice, thesis words, the place where the sentence turns. A sidenote2 is how the reader is given the footnote without being asked to look down. They sit on the same page; they never share a phrase.
Skim · numbered
Every Monday at 09:00 ET, the digest arrives — verified, signed, archived3. The standup happens anyway, but it is fifteen minutes shorter.
Hot · ! marker
Billed by delivery!. If the outcome doesn't ship, no line.
With italic author voice
The humanist computer is the insight that these two dispositions — craft and machinery — are not opposed. The machine handles the cadence4. The prose handles the voice5.
Rule · placement Use sidenotes for context the reader can opt into — the source of a number, the caveat behind a claim, the operator who built the responsibility. Never to repeat the prose.
Rule · the ! Use the bang marker only when the sentence will not survive without context — the kind of caveat a careful reader would ask after. Two bangs on one page loses the warning.
Rule · layering Italic and sidenote never share a phrase. The writer marks; the reader is offered. Crossing them puts the reader's voice in the writer's mouth.
Rule · the mark Never on the logotype. Never on a button. The sidenote is a prose tool only — body copy, editorial H2/H3, document preview headers. Not chrome.
IV · Editorial Pairing

Set in type — the voice at length, with a reader's margin.

The Tufte voice is calm, specific, and unafraid of a short sentence. It names things by their nouns. It reaches for italic only when italic is warranted. And it leaves room in the margin for the reader to disagree, ask, or check.
From the field notes

On the quiet arrival of Monday reports.

Once, the week began with a scramble1. Someone opened seven dashboards, pulled the numbers, wrote the digest, and dropped it in the 09:00 standup. The rotation was a line in the budget. The standup was a line in the calendar. Nobody was quite sure what either accomplished beyond confirming that the dashboards were fine.

1 Pre-OpenProse, this rotation cost the company forty operator-hours a quarter. The cost line read “Monday brief.”

Then the outcome was declared in a file. Fifteen lines.2 Every Monday at 09:00 ET, the digest arrives — verified, signed, archived3. The standup happens anyway, but it is fifteen minutes shorter. Nobody asks where the numbers came from. They are already in the document.

2 The file is YAML. The whole spec fits on a postcard. See the formal spec in §VII.
3 Verification is the part that actually changed. The numbers were always automated; the trust was not.

The first thing we noticed was what we stopped doing — the standing meetings where we'd review the dashboard to confirm the dashboard was fine.

The humanist computer4 is the insight that these two dispositions — craft and machinery — are not opposed. The machine handles the cadence. The prose handles the voice. The page reads the way a good analyst would have written it. The result is the point!.

4 The phrase is borrowed from approach-J's logotype study. The opposition between “humanist” and “computer” is the false one we are setting down.
! The bang marker reserved for the page's single load-bearing claim. If you find yourself adding a second, the first is wrong.

V · Logo Variations

One mark, three impressions.

VIII · Voice & Tone

Three sentences, one register, three marks.

Three sanctioned voices the brand uses: the short declaration, the spec-sheet note, the editorial observation. Each sentence can live on its own without ornament — and each demonstrates one placement of the sidenote.
Voice · short · numbered
“Write the outcome. We run it.1”

The tagline. Six words. The sidenote, in the marketing context, expands “we run it” into the verbs the operator wants confirmed: verify, deliver, archive.

Voice · spec · bang
“Billed by delivery!. If the outcome doesn't ship, no line.”

Terms of art spoken plainly. The bang marker on a single word — the one that defines the pricing model — is the strongest mark in the system. One per page.

Voice · editorial · italic
“Once, the week began with a scramble. Then the outcome was declared in a file.”

The long form voice. Italic carries the pivot. No sidenote here — this sentence is still the author's, not yet the reader's.
