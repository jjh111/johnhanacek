II · Type Scale

Cormorant Garamond for the headline. Inter for the body and chrome.

A two-face system, inverted from α: where α uses Palatino for both display and body, ζ splits the work. Cormorant Garamond — light at 300, regular at 400, italic for emphasis — carries every headline, blockquote, and feature card title. Inter handles every paragraph, label, button, and badge. The split is the SaaS convention: serif elegance for the moments the reader pauses on; sans clarity for the work in between. Italic is always steel blue.

V · The Steel & The Tint

Italic steel is the writer. The steel pill is the reader. The warm tint is the comparison.

ζ runs three layered marks. Italic steel-blue is the author's emphasis — the same role italic-red plays in α, just in a different ink. The steel-tint pill on a serif phrase is the reader's pause — soft enough to scan past, present enough to come back to. The warm-tint pill is reserved for the "before" half of a comparison — the old way, the manual hours, the line item being eliminated. Together they make the page look like a SaaS product brief that has been carefully edited.

Rule · steel pill Use on the nouns and numbers a busy operator would scroll back to. Steel pill is a soft 8% wash on a serif phrase — never an underline, never a fluorescent block.
Rule · warm tint Use only on the "before" half of a comparison — the old way, the manual hours, the legacy SLA. The warm tint signals what the product is replacing, not what the product is.
Rule · layering Italic steel and the steel pill never share a phrase — one is the writer, one is the reader. The warm tint and the steel pill never share a sentence — one is the past, one is the present.
Rule · the mark Never on the logotype. Never on a button. Pills are prose tools only — body copy, editorial H2/H3, comparison cards. Not chrome.
VI · Editorial Pairing

Set in type — the voice at length, with a steel pen.

The SaaS voice is calm, specific, and clearly approachable. Cormorant carries the headline; Inter carries the running paragraph. The italic steel marks the pivot. The steel pill marks the reader's pause. Read aloud, the cadence is one a marketing-page reader recognizes — problem, declaration, evidence, ask — but each sentence carries the weight of a sentence that wants to be read.

From the field notes

On the quiet arrival of Monday reports.

Once, the week began with a scramble. Someone opened seven dashboards, pulled the numbers, wrote the digest, and dropped it in the 09:00 standup. The rotation was a line in the budget. The standup was a line in the calendar. Nobody was quite sure what either accomplished beyond confirming that the dashboards were fine.

Then the outcome was declared in a file. Fifteen lines. Every Monday at 09:00 ET, the digest arrives — verified, signed, archived. The standup happens anyway, but it is fifteen minutes shorter. Nobody asks where the numbers came from. They are already in the document.

The first thing we noticed was what we stopped doing — the standing meetings where we'd review the dashboard to confirm the dashboard was fine.

The humanist computer is the insight that these two dispositions — craft and machinery — are not opposed. The machine handles the cadence. The prose handles the voice. The page reads the way a good analyst would have written it. The result is the point.

Maya Chen · Head of Operations · Series-C payments platform

VIII · Voice & Tone

Three sentences, one register, three marks.

Three sanctioned voices the brand uses: the short declaration, the spec-sheet note, the editorial observation. Each sentence can live on its own without ornament — and each demonstrates one placement of the steel pill.

Voice · short · steel pill
“Write the outcome. We run it.”

The tagline. Six words. The pill lands on the second sentence — the part that shifts the burden from the operator to the service.

Voice · spec · warm tint
“Billed by delivery. If the outcome doesn't ship, no line.”

Terms of art spoken plainly. Italic steel on the load-bearing word; warm tint on the contrast clause — the part that says “unlike the legacy SaaS, this is what we do not charge for.”

Voice · editorial · italic
“Once, the week began with a scramble. Then the outcome was declared in a file.”

The long form voice. Italic steel carries the pivot. No pill here — this sentence is still the author's, not yet the reader's.
