# [Substrate for the Future](https://blogs.commons.georgetown.edu/cctp-820-fall2015/substrate-for-the-future/)

Reading accounts of the formation of the principles of the Internet design, I was greatly impressed by the simultaneous foresight and humility of the design thinking involved. The designers approaching the concept of networking computers together did not make specific claims about what the network may or may not be able to do, but instead focused entirely on building a substrate that would scale. This resulted in a network design that was never about a totality, but was always about meshing together many different systems under one conceptual paradigm: the packet switched network.

As the concept of the Internet continues to evolve, I found that much of the “modern” or even “futuristic” talk of Internet of Things and the need for cryptography and accounting was much older than I realized.

The differences between Davies’ goals of usability and Baran’s goals of prioritizing survival and efficacy of time sharing led to very different systems. Indeed, I was struck that Baran had proposed a “priority system that would allow high-level users to preempt messages from lower-level users.” (Abbate, 11) This is exactly what the modern conversations of “net neutrality” are all about: the efficacy of the ability to sorting and forwarding functions to communications switching over the network based on hierarchies. Advocates of net neutrality do not want such hierarchies and worry that ISPs already have the ability to sort traffic if they are not checked by law. It is interesting to note that such sorting was being considered in the very earliest days of the design of computer networks. This tension between existing powers getting the best use of the service and keeping the service neutral for all parties has been in the Internet from the start it seems.

Also of extreme interest to me was to learn that that the Mark I network that Davies headed up was essentially the Internet of Things long before that concept became reified as the “next great step” for the Internet. “The network was actually interposed between a computer and its own peripherals, so that the network became, in a sense, internal to the computer.” (Abbate, 32) This is essentially the goal of the designs for Internet of Things (IoT) protocols and approaches. The network becomes not something that the things connect to, but something that connects the things. The Mark I paradigm did not “win” in the initial days, that was ARPANET. The design of ARPANET was much more interested in linking facilities than in linking individual devices, owing to the scale of the military thinking that supported and drove it.

Thus in the 80s and 90s we had an Internet that was treated  a thing: the information superhighway, the net, the web, etc

However, now we seem to be coming back to a Mark I conception of Internet, not as a thing we connect to, but as a substrate that suspends all that needs connecting. The Android/Apple smartphone began this change for us as individuals: instead of sitting in a room to “log in” to the Internet, we quickly found ourselves in a situation where the Internet is all around us. In the 90s the paradigm was to log in. Now people desire to log out. To paraphrase Floridi, we are enveloping our societal activities in an information sphere, where connection is always possible and indeed often sustained.

Yet of course as we have seen in this class, the actual implementation of technological systems does not always carry the idealism of the initial approaches. Most striking was comparing Licklider’s 1960 conception of human brains coupled with computing and the actual realization of this. His was a vision of increased intellectualism and creativity, and I have experienced in my childhood that much of this is true. Exposure to the information available on the Internet and the affordances of digital media made me who I am, and I feel grateful that I grew up in the time when the conception of networked computers was an actionable availability. Today I still use my smartphone to learn about the world and experiment with art. Yet this is not inherent in the design, I choose to learn.

The vision of computers in symbiosis with human minds seems beautiful when many designs are mostly being shaped by academics and people committed to learning. Now many designs are being shaped by capital and people committed to tricking consumers. So instead of a network that makes finding psychological support from sympathetic communities, or helps surface relevant knowledge trees to follow, we now find ourselves in a network that has evolved ever better at letting us pay for things and distract ourselves.

Even the initial militaristic beginnings have seemed to burn off in favor of an Internet devoted to capitalism and consumerism. When we learn about the cloud we will see how centralized models have re-asserted themselves (and I will explain how even a distributed hardware network can function as a centralized conceptual network).

The tensions between all the users of the Internet have been so well served by the substrate of switched packets.  Design for change and the creation of “tussle spaces” has served so far, but we will need to think more deeply about the nature of the packets themselves. To me, the blockchain represents a rich space of experimentation in how to make modularized databases. I think the concepts of “communication” “computing” and “database” have the potential to merge into a packet-ized version of storage itself. The Ethereum project takes this further and is exploring if a packet-based metaphor can serve as a substrate for applications themselves. Could we create dynamic packets that are each self-contained turing-complete computing modules that run on the ambient “ether” of total available computing?

I think the next stages of Internet will see interesting intersections between packets and computing itself. It seems like Davies’ “design for interactivity” is getting ready to take the stage from Baran’s “design for survivability”. Tension lines are apparent: is the Internet another place, or is it enveloping place? Should we be allowed anonymity in the space between computers, or should that space remember the world it is built from?

There are no easy answers, and if history is any indication, many ideas will merge to make the actual solutions, while many other ideas will never quite spread to the edges.
