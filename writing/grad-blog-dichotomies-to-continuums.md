# [From Dichotomies to Continuums – Moving the Myth Space](https://blogs.commons.georgetown.edu/cctp-820-fall2015/from-dichotomies-to-continuums-moving-the-myth-space/)

The modern story continues to play out as technology vs. society. This is a myth.

Technology is society made durable, as Latour writes, “think of technology as congealed labor.” Then you can begin to see that society is its technologies and technologies emerge within social context.

We have to remember that technology is not just computers, but the room our class meets in, the clothes we’re wearing, the food in our stomachs which was grown in the sociotechnical web of agriculture, farmers, domesticated animals, and tractors, delivered by road network to store networks, and so on.

The word technology has its roots in the word technique, which refers to a way of carrying out a particular task. We begin to bundle techniques into relational networks and then blackbox them into technological artifacts. “when? ever we learn something about the management of humans, we shift that knowledge to nonhumans and endow them with more and more organizational properties.” (Latour, 207) So that social structures like Facebook emerge as made from a vast web of the human participants, software agents running tasks, programmers maintaining and adding features and so on. Each of the participants is themselves embedded in a sociotechnical agency web. Even older forms like the town square are a sociotechnical web, a built environment where humans and nonhumans interact.

Technology does not force us to do things directly, instead it expands/contracts the possibility space of our choices. We still have choices. A car makes us fatter if we drive to get fast food and fitter if we drive to the gym. Skype lets us stay in our rooms and connect with other minds but never touch their bodies, or it holds us over between our next reunion in local space. Society is a growing constellation of distributed agency. A human-gun system can have a modified goal than a human-knife system, or a human-pillow system. Still there are choices, it’s just that the artifact can modify the possibility space of those choices. All three human systems I just described can kill. Only one is particularly comfortable to sleep on.

Even the language I am using to convey my thoughts to you now is a technique, a system of structuring thinking into a grammar that can be encoded in temporal sequences of words that are received by your ears if the medium is air-molecule vibration or your minds ear if you are reading, and decompressed back into thoughts. Language is system and process. When it is externalized on paper and recreated by machinations we begin to call it technology. But tools for thinking are tools just the same. Technologies spring from the grammar of the thinkers who imagine them, and the imagination of thinkers is based on their experiences in the world around them. The world around us is a sociotechnical one. So our grammars are path dependent upon the context of our environment.

The divide between society and technology is a myth, and an obfuscation of the complex rippling networks of inter-relational agency. We can drop it and adopt the idea of sociotechnical. Now though, we have another hard job ahead of us: we have to navigate the false divide of sociotechnical and natural. This is lurking below visibility. Technologists and sociologists can become united, but now it’s time to talk to the physicists and the biologists.

Brian Arthur is helping us here. By defining technology as the harnessing of phenomena for purposes he is breaking down a false wall between nature and sociotechnical artifacts. Yet he stopped short by saying technology was harnessed phenomena for human purposes. Instead technology is a continuum of harnessed phenomena and enmeshed technique. So that chimpanzees configure social machines made of each other to build societies even though they do not have technological artifacts as do. Dolphins pass down hunting techniques to their young. This is culture, it just has not been made durable in artifact. There is a continuum here where once we thought of a dichotomy. Nature and sociotechnical humanity are still part of one universe.

Look at the human path. Once we drew on cave walls lit by firelight. Now we watch youtube videos streamed over fiberoptic cables. In both cases we have natural materials and phenomena repurposed to achieve a goal. Clays and wood and the phenomena of fire, to electricity, light, silicon and glass. As Latour articulates, the youtube stories are different in the complexity of the mesh they required to build and meshes they require to maintain. Fire was used to heat the glass to make the fiberoptic cables. The core natural phenomena are still present.

The fundamental act is no different: we are using the world to tell stories about our experiences and imaginations, which are situated in the world.

There are no hard lines between the fluctuations of quantum field dynamics, the chattering correspondence of neurons in the brain, the built environment of sociotechnical reality, and the solar-earth magnetic connection. Even cosmic rays from the deepest reaches of spacetime can scramble information in the DNA of organisms and lead to new mutations. All is part of a whole, all is interrelated and correlated. It is not enough to stop at sociotechnical agency webs, we have to keep going. We have to look at scales far smaller and larger than we typically do. Even our brain has nested networks of distributed agency, with neurons configuring local connections and Schwann cells strengthening or weakening those connections.

We have to understand that selection pressures are a feature of the universe and that sociotechnical systems are part of nature, that they are made of nature. There never was modernity. There never was a cleft from nature. Instead we have only participated in the continual evolution of matter and energy into new informatics. The selection pressures of survival in a food-scare world saw our ancestors begin agriculture. The selection pressures of survival in a business world see us developing faster computers. Still selection pressures remain. The webs of agency create environments, which echo back into our webs of agency. There is not free will, there is constrained will. Agency exists, but it is a negotiation not just between agents but between the possibility space of the environment.

The sociotechnical must become the sociotechnicalnatural; and we will need a new word to hold this understanding.
