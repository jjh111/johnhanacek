# [Virtual Reality – From Metamedium to Metaverse](https://blogs.commons.georgetown.edu/cctp-820-fall2015/virtual-reality-from-metamedium-to-metaverse/)

I just got back from a journey yesterday where I went to some amazing little pocket universes. You can go to: up at the Savoy Suites on Wisconsin just above Calvert St, there’s a team demoing the HTC Vive Virtual reality headset until Thursday. It has two controllers that are tracked and which serve as your hands in the space. The demos are fun, from being on the bow of a sunken ship, to a very cheeky “work 2050” job simulator, to a beautifully usable 3D painting application. I drew ribbons of light all around me. Not around my avatar. I wasn’t seeing my creations through a portal, I was inside of the world where my creations live. When you go into VR you have traveled to another universe: the universe of digital affordance.

I want to impress some distinctions between interacting with a computer inside VR and interacting with a computer through a 2D screen; what follows is an attempt to tease out the largest differences and run with them a bit to see where we might be headed in experience design. Because VR is the most visceral experience it is currently possible to have with computer-generated content. It is also very difficult to describe with video because video is a conceptually “smaller” medium than VR.

Videos cannot show virtual reality, but virtual reality can show videos. Please understand: virtual reality is conceptually larger than current all current medium channels. VR can be a universe itself, and you are not looking in, but rather inside of that universe.

https://www.youtube.com/embed/0Djt9i3ZKZE?feature=oembed

The idea of the computer as metamedium expands very dramatically in a VR paradigm. Now not just other media can be reproduced functionally (the abstracted computer notebook, the music player interface, etc), but entire 3D artifacts. Even entire realities themselves. Entire 3D computer artifacts can be rendered inside of the space. So now in the VR metamedium we can render the metamedium devices of traditional 2D computers. These computational artifacts become themselves modules to recombine, and so in VR we see the dawning of new possibilities.

Traditional 2D computer screens are something like a closed glass door. We are separated by the door yet can look in. On the other side there can be any numerous things, from text to entire 3D worlds. Currently though, we are looking through the door at these other worlds; we have yet to walk through into them.

The act of donning a VR headset? is like opening the glass door and walking through into the digital content. Now “content” is no longer an apt description- it has become experience.

“In digital as in mechanical design, our goal should be to remove distraction, to allow the user to focus on the task at hand rather than the tool, by making the interface elements transparent, and allowing the interactor direct manipulation.” – Englebart

Where on a 2D computer this is very abstracted, in VR it becomes much more literal: in VR your body can come with you. Currently it is limited to hands tracked by controllers.  
![Vive controller](../Assets/grad/img14.png)  
The HTC system uses two controllers that are tracked by lasers. The controllers have trigger buttons for each hand and thumbpads for each hand, along with a squeeze grip for each hand. The tracking of the controllers was essentially perfect: where my proprioception (body sense) felt my hands were in analog space is where my hands were in VR space.

The future paradigm can feature one’s whole body; the Kinect that was designed by Jaron Lanier’s lab to bring a user’s whole body into the VR space. Computer vision techniques and even jewelry or suits can be employed to digitize your kinesthetic actions in a 1:1 mapping of analog to virtual.

As Murray highlights, the computer is a participatory medium, thus we have an expectation of interaction and it is frustrating to us when we cannot interact with a computer software. In VR things are weirder. Reality is a participatory medium, and Virtual reality evokes the feeling of being in a reality. So you get the urge to touch everything. With controllers this is limited. With traditional programs this is also limited- the world will not react to you in the natural way that the analog reality does, the programmer has to build in interaction.

I wanted to touch and interact with every part of the virtual worlds I was in. Yet I could tell which areas were interactive and which were not.  I can poke a hole in an analog wall, I cannot do that in a digital wall; unless it has been programmed. I went to open a drawer in one of the demo experiences and that drawer did not respond analogously to my speed of opening, but rather triggered a scripted event that opened the drawer at a certain speed. This was frustrating in VR in a way that I have never experienced in video games before. I wanted control not just of triggering the action, but also control of the way the action was carried out.

After this most recent VR experience I can see a trajectory – a branch – of programming that will mature out of the simulation paradigm: we’ll start making realities with physics based on interrelated primitives rather than “large” scripted events.

As Murray briefly mentions, we are seeing simulations with emergent behaviors. This hints at a future of programming beyond crafting explicit logical operations and toward creating messy ecologies that interact and lead to unpredictable behavior.

As we begin to experience computer software not as a metamedium but as a metaverse we will make increasing use of simulations with emergent phenomena. It would be impossible to explicitly name every interactive variable. So instead we can govern macro rules that manipulate correlated voxels. So if I interact with a voxelized metaenvironment it will respond with a kind of finer-grained “physics” rather than a clunky scripted if/then sequence.

Murray talks about simulations as ecologies of variables, and these digital ecologies will grow immensely once we can experience them as we are used to experiencing analog ecologies: as embodied animals. We will see an explosion of complexity in simulations as researchers and nonexperts alike begin to travel inside of simulations and explore them, rather than just accept the output readouts from the model’s runtime. Today we have inputs, run the simulation, and read outputs; in the near future we will experience simulations as versions of reality. Indeed, a future profession will emerge of “knowledge ecologists”, those who explore simulations in an analogous way that we explore analog ecosystems.

This will not destroy traditional programming, rather it is a branch of the computational endeavor. There is a rich field of cellular automata that von Neumann contributed much to, and which allows “programmers” to set in motion chains of events that unfold over time in only semi-predicable ways. I urge you to try it yourself by playing [The Game of Life](http://www.bitstorm.org/gameoflife/).

Additionally, I must impress upon you the new notions of scalar relationship to information that are possible inside VR. Looking at a model of the Golden Gate bridge on a 2D computer renders the giant object very small, the size of the viewport essentially. In VR the bridge regains its “true” relative size and if you get close you must look up to see it.

The concept of human-centered design takes on new meaning in VR. Inside VR you are the center of the experience. Since VR is a metamedium the first things we will bring inside are likely existing forms; however these forms will have a twist. For example, the painting application I was inside of had a palette on my left hand and a brush/selector on my right hand. I pointed my right hand at my left hand to select the brush parameters, then painted with my right hand in space. The controllers don’t afford typing since you must grip them, so solving text input in such a system will be an interesting challenge. Indeed inside of VR you have to bring every device that you want to use into the virtual reality. The headset cuts you off from the world, so if you want a keyboard in VR one will have to be rendered inside the environment. If you want to bring your phone with you it would have to be rendered inside the virtual space as a simulation of the artifact.

“Space and time are the two foundational coordinates of human cognition” – Murray

With VR headsets, the spatial affordance of the computer is expanded. Now instead of abstractly perceiving web pages as sites that we visit, one can much more literally visit a web site. Through an environment like JanusVR, one can navigate and construct 3D rooms that are filled with information, while themselves being made of information. So we can retrieve our ability to remember the locations of objects in space, yet now we are in a metaverse where structures can be created that are themselves abstractions. The form and content are more meaningfully merged. So instead of a browsing history being a list of previous 2D planar pages, it becomes the room three doors behind you. The web can add the ability to be experienced as a kind of hyper-museum, since unlike analog reality, virtual reality can have a hyperlink to another world as a door.

“There is a naïve belief that we can teach just through talking and listening.” – Alan Kay

The past and current focus on talking is why VR excites me so much: with VR we can start communicating to the proper channels to engage meaningful learning, not just memorization or word-abusing. Inside virtual reality we can craft synesthetic experiences that are an order of magnitude more engaging than has ever been possible before. There has never been a medium like VR other than experience itself. The VR headset is mediating your access to the digital environments, but it is doing so in a way that wraps around your perception like no screen has ever done. When you step through the door into VR your visual and auditory system is somewhere else. All my memories in VR have a visceral spatial encoding –  I remember where the thing was relative to me. So when I have flown on a dragon’s back, or painted in light around me, these are my experiences, they are not media as I have ever known them.

This is fundamentally different than viewing such 3D content on a 2D screen. We have had 3D content for decades now, but no good way to enter into that 3D world. Now we do. Rather than staring at the planar surface of the door, we can step inside and experience the 3D worlds we create with computers.

This will enable entirely new affordances in education. We can create spaces where students can embody concepts, such as Jaron Lanier’s work with using Kinect to have kids make their bodies into the shapes of molecules. Not just visualization, not just imagining you are there, but a much more literal embodiment.

“If you want people to go along with you, you have to involve them in the same conspiracy.” – Alan Kay

Or as I’ve heard it said most recently, “usability trumps persuasion.” In a virtual reality environment the teacher and students can all gather together in a shared learning space and go on adventures together through the concepts.

“if you don’t have a good theory of learning you can still get it to happen by just helping a person focus.” – Alan Kay

You do this by removing interference, as Kay continues. In interacting with the computer we do not want to interact with the computer, we want to interact with the information that we are engaged with. So having a computer can become a drawback for certain kinds of engagement.

As the woman learning tennis said, “when you just stop thinking your body knows what to do.” We have an incredible ability to navigate 3D worlds over time. Current computers might be simple enough to use, and can “get out of our way” at times, but current computers do not harness our deeper kinesthetic and synthesehtic abilities. This is changing. The future of computing is the future of experience itself. We have been standing at the door – with some researchers walking through. Soon we can all walk through.

VR inspires visions of a future where we can utilize the bodily forms that we live in to also learn in. A future where the synesthetic and kinesthetic experiences of “genius” people can become shared environments for teach/learn education. It will be a blending, indeed 2D content represented in VR will still exist. Still, instead of sitting in chairs and looking at screens filled with wonderful content, we can now step through the screen and participate in the content as a reality rather than mere media. The concept of metamedium is growing to encompass not just media, but the concept of reality itself. The computational metamedium is becoming a metaverse, and it will be incredible to see what we can learn and build together in virtual spaces.
