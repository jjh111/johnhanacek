# [Internet Appliance](https://blogs.commons.georgetown.edu/cctp-820-fall2015/internet-appliance/)

The modern tablet and smartphone are Internet Appliances. Particularly exemplified by the Apple instantiations, the modern Internet appliance is less about computing as it is about hiding the fact that it is a computer. Instead the modern Internet Appliance is primarily a conduit for the consumption of pre-packaged content and services. “But we can create on tablets,” someone may say. This creation is illusory. The Internet Appliance cannot create executable programs for itself using itself. It requires another more computer-y computer to accomplish this. There are powerful creative affordances to touch screen devices, this is true. However, they are not computers unless they can be employed by the user to create new executable programs. Instead the Internet Appliance is based around the concept of “apps” self-contained compiled software packages with their The iOS modality is the extreme case. The only way users can modify the functionality of a given iOS device is to install a siloed app that has the functionality they are looking for. The Apple Appliances even remove the possibility of navigating and modifying macro-level hierarchies in the file system. This becomes problematic when the database blocks of each app begin to fill up and the user has no easy way to clear these caches–there is no trash can on an iPad.

The Internet Appliance is the result of converging desires on the part of computer users and computer manufacturers. Users wanted the affordances of computing without the computer, and manufacturers wanted greater control over what the user could do. These are complementary when we consider the history of computer users.

Computer users in the days of the first PCs were usually programmers themselves, people interested in adding functionality to what was essentially a blank-slate. Pre-GUI, the metaphor space of the PC was very small, instead it relied upon users to speak esoteric computer languages and assemble strings of commands to explicitly tell the computer what to do and in what order. With the GUI came the visual metaphor space of file menus, file systems, the desktop all interacted with via a pointing device. This allowed non-programmers to use their visual intuitions and causal memory to explore the computer as a kind of environment rather than explicitly knowing what they wanted to achieve beforehand. Combined with object-oriented programming concepts the user/programmer divide was lessened as complex software packages could be assembled from modular blocks of interface and behavior code.

The GUI opened the computer to many more kinds of users, thus the amount of affordances the computer allowed expanded exponentially. From business to art, the computer and software afford the possibility of an imagination space where trial and error are virtually costless.

The PC user base filled up with “normal” people for whom the computer was not a device that manipulated signs and symbols using boolean logic, but rather the box they draw pictures on, or the box they type papers on, or later the box they use to look at cat pictures, and etc. Computer users began to understand that computers become more powerful in processing speed and get more refined in their “usability.”

The Internet Appliance was a next step in the direction of usability. By acknowledging that most people were not using the computer but rather many software layers above “computing”, Apple and Android (bought by Google) created an operating environment where the use metaphor became even simpler: the pointing device is your finger. As well, these new operating systems hid the core “computer-y” elements from the user so that they could not inadvertently break the internal logic of the linked code objects that make the operating system run.

Yet the manufacturers had another primary motive for limiting user meddling potential: the profit motive. Once the computer has been black-boxed into an appliance, the user is now much more at the mercy of other programmers. On a tablet itself there is no clear pathway from using programs to creating programs, there is no good software writing/compiling affordance on an Internet Appliance. On a PC there is.

Users have gained usability at the expense of freedom. PCs still do exist, and still allow the installation and compilation of any new software conceivable with no barriers other than potential system errors and/or time, but the success of Internet Appliances has seen users move toward the appliance paradigm and away from the computer paradigm. The manufacturers are seen speeding this transition along, as now users are limited as to what software they can install and what they can do with the device they purchased. Apple’s opaque database management system for its apps means that unwary users may find themselves buying new devices to “fix” slow performance rather than learning how to empty the trash, because there is no real ability to do so. Deleting the entire app, preferences and all, every time one needs to free up space is hostile. It signals the user that the real solution is to buy much more space than they need just to account for app bloat. The profit motive has seen manufacturers take advantage of the closed nature of the appliance to steer users into entire services ecosystems. With the iOS devices the user is forbidden by design to engage with the full affordances of services other than Apple’s own. The same for Android.

Apple is the leading vanguard of the “it just works” modality of computing. The user should not have to think about what is going on in order to do what they are trying to do. This is admirable, but I argue, has now gotten out of hand. The profit motive saw the inclusion of system-wide advertisements at a level very near the kernel of the iOS operating system. The iAd system has the potential to freeze user inputs until the ad is engaged with. This is a non-free computing environment. Google, Microsoft and Apple are all selling devices that are increasingly easier to understand while being increasingly hostile to user freedom. There is a better compromise, but the profit motive has created a feedback loop where all the major manufacturers are now following the same formula: use carrot/stick methods to lure users in then constrain user choice into proprietary ecosystems through base-level design of the systems. Increasingly we do not have general purpose computers on the market, but rather competing appliances with proprietary design aesthetic and features. I shudder to think of this trend continuing into wearable computers and hybrid reality modalities.
