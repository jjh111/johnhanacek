# [Sounding the Never Alarm](https://blogs.commons.georgetown.edu/cctp-820-fall2015/sounding-the-never-alarm/)

Uh oh, Ken said “never” now I have to sound the “never alarm”. thanks Ken, geez

But this is not just toward Ken, but is toward anyone who doubts the possibility that computers could be constructed to have higher-level cognitive abilities. Thinking is physical, and so is computing.

It is useful to think about computing in the abstract, but it feels like it’s my job in this class to remind us to consider that computers are physical systems, made of atoms holding energy. Every computer program, while running, is real. It is dictating the movements of energy units (electrons mostly) through atomic lattices. When you program a computer, you are programming a corner of the universe that was made into a computer. (Lloyd)

Remembering our physics lets us not forget that human minds are also physical. They are worlds more complex than our most advanced “neural net” metaphorical computer things. Yet the physicality of computing and the physicality of human cognition should give us pause. The shared, if specifically different, physical basis of computers and humans is enough for me to resist saying never to the idea that computers may think.

If we continue to understand what “computing” actually is, then we find a deeply physical reality to the execution of seemingly abstract and linguistic programs. A turing machine is not very helpful when it’s running in your imagination, but when it is physical you can bounce your imagination off it. The machine will keep running long after you have slept. Only by making computing tangible outside of our minds does it gain the immense power that we attribute to it.

So from gears to electron gates humans have found ways to arrange atoms into systems that they can be programmed with human logic. It’s amazing really.

Now we learned in the first half of the class that, essentially, humans program other humans. Senders create strings of symbols that elicit reactions on the part of the receiver. This is of course taking place in an incalculable mesh network of transceivers, not the simple sender/receiver loop metaphor.

So human-human programming is fuzzy. As all of us found in doing python exercises human-computer programming is not allowed to be fuzzy.

Or is it? As other humans have learned more about the physical affordances allowing us to create computers and the physical nature of brains, parallels are being drawn in new engineered systems. Now there are approaches that allow “programming” to happen not through explicit commands, but through the repetition of semi-structured inputs, such as the frames of video. The relationship lattices that we call (undeservedly) “neural nets” are early proto-examples of “computers” that are being programmed with fuzzy inputs.

HOWEVER, these computers are still operating at a base level of 0/1, they are still flipping digital transistors. As we learn more about human brains we are coming to see that they have much more than mere transistors. Human brains have hundreds of different kinds of cells. A modern “neural net” computer is comprised of neurons that are all identical, but respond to one another so as to allow patterns to form through continued input.

A human brain is comprised of neurons that are each somewhat autonomous. Our minds are made of little animals.

Could we build computers with such a paradigm? Better said: could we build systems that behave like our brains? Nothing in physics is preventing it. The possibility is there and so is the will.

So if you say “never” to a human-made device exhibiting fuzziness, maybe even exhibiting creativity, then you were likely the same kind of person who would have said “never” to the idea that humans would ever fly.

Just sayin’ be careful with that “never” word, since you cannot control its meaning.
